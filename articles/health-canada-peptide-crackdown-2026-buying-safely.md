## What did Health Canada actually announce?

Across 2026, Health Canada published a series of public advisories on canada.ca warning that specific companies were selling unauthorized injectable peptide products — drugs that had not been assessed by Health Canada for safety, efficacy, or quality, and were not approved for human use. The affected product lists named compounds like BPC-157, CJC-1295, Ipamorelin, Sermorelin, Retatrutide, Tirzepatide, and HGH — sold and marketed for direct human injection rather than labeled and restricted to laboratory research use.

Coverage from CBC News and Global News confirmed at least one case escalated to a permanent injunction, and the College of Physicians and Surgeons of Alberta separately warned members about the health risks of unauthorized injectable peptides reaching patients.

## Why does "unauthorized" matter here?

The distinction Health Canada is drawing isn't about peptides as a research category — it's about how a product is tested, labeled, and marketed. A seller crosses into "unauthorized drug" territory when it markets an unapproved compound for human therapeutic use without going through Health Canada's drug approval process. Selling the same compound labeled strictly for research use, tested by an independent lab, and never marketed with human-use claims is a materially different risk profile.

## The checklist: what actually separates a compliant source from a risky one

| Signal | Lower risk | Red flag |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lab testing | Independent third-party (e.g. Janoshik) | Seller's own in-house numbers only |
| Lot matching | COA lot number matches the vial received | Generic COA, no lot verification |
| Labeling | Explicit "research use only," no dosing-for-humans language | Marketed with human dosing/injection instructions |
| Claims | No therapeutic/medical claims | Promises of treatment, cure, or guaranteed results |

- Ask for the Certificate of Analysis before you buy, not after.

- Confirm the testing lab is independent — a seller grading its own homework isn't verification.

- Check that the lot number on the COA matches what's printed on your vial.

- Walk away from any listing that talks about human dosing, treatment, or medical outcomes.
